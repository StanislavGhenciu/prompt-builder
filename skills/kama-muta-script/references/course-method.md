## 1. The problem (`00:00:00–00:14:20`)
- The biggest challenge today is breaking through the noise. Content supply is an endless ocean; AI is turning it into a tsunami of "slop that lacks the one thing that truly matters, a human soul" (`00:04:21`).
- The answer is to go against the trend: stop chasing hacks and go back to the craft of storytelling plus the science of psychology. The aim is content people actually want to watch, "content that entertains, that offers value, and content that tells a truth."
- "The world doesn't need more content. It needs better stories." (`00:08:54`)
- Opening film: *The Unsung Heroes of Science* (Royal DSM). See course-films.md.

## 2. Strategy (`00:14:21–00:38:11`)
- **One KPI: reputation.** Its most important driver (per RepTrak) is emotional appeal: "I trust / admire / feel connected to / respect this organization" (`00:15:58`). Reputation decides whether stakeholders spend their time, money and trust on you.
- **"Touch the heart and the mind will open."** (McKee, *Storynomics*; `00:17:18`). For 99.9% of the world, minds are closed, skeptical and indifferent. An emotional experience is the key that unlocks that door.
- **Kama muta** (`00:18:43–00:21:05`). Alan Fiske, psychological anthropologist at UCLA, defines it as the emotion "when a communal sharing relationship either is created, begins, or suddenly gets stronger." Kama = love, muta = moved.
  - Signs: a warm, fuzzy feeling in the chest, goosebumps, tears.
  - The moment is short, "10 seconds, a minute", but "the motives that grow out of it can last … a lifetime."
- **Definition used by the course:** a *sudden intensification of a sense of connection*. Examples (`00:20:24–00:21:17`): a stadium erupting when the team scores, a concert when the beat drops, a surprise party, astronauts seeing Earth.
- **Third person** (`00:21:19`): "you can also experience kamamuta in the 3rd person when you see someone else experiencing that connection." That is why storytelling is "the single most effective tool we have to create kamamuta at scale."
- **Reunion and redemption plots** work particularly well. Both imply a sudden intensification of connectedness at the end: physically (reunion) or metaphorically (a wrong made right).
- **The third element is story–message fit** (`00:22:08–00:25:25`). Renault Clio, "30 years in the making", is a beautiful story whose payoff is "just a lazy statement based on the most superficial connection to the story." Even "Bringing people together for 30 years" would have made more sense.
- **The study** (`00:25:26–00:27:25`). One Camera worked with the University of Amsterdam. Hundreds of viewers watched corporate films and answered validated surveys measuring kama muta, narrative transportation, story-message fit, reputation and social engagement intent.
  - Result: storytelling that elicits kama muta *and* has good message fit has a direct, positive, statistically significant effect on reputation. The strongest effect is on emotional appeal, but every reputation dimension rises.
  - It also massively increases social engagement.
- **Formula** (`00:27:26`): "A true story, well-told, that moves people to kamomuta and has a message that connects authentically and emotionally will measurably increase your reputation."
- **Cost** (`00:28:07–00:35:06`). The cost per viewer should be under 50 cents. Big budgets are not required: the highest kama muta score in the study went to Canon's documentary portrait (*A Maternidade*), which cost about 15K including travel, and could be done below 10K.
- **Ethics** (`00:35:07–00:36:42`). "With great power comes great responsibility." Storytelling must not manipulate or mislead; "it must be used to uplift and to tell the truth." McKee signs every book "Write the truth." "If the story we tell isn't rooted in some form of honesty and vulnerability, it will never work."
- The corporate world "seems to be designed to destroy true storytelling". Four enemies follow, and "knowing how to tell a story starts by knowing how not to tell a story" (`00:36:42–00:38:02`).

## 3. Messaging (`00:38:12–00:50:13`)
- **Symptom.** The message stops being the passenger and becomes the whole vehicle: over-explaining, repetition, every talking point crammed in, "until there's no room left for the story to actually breathe." The more you push the message, the more you alienate the audience.
- "Your audience is not interested in your message. Your audience, however, is interested in a good story." A good story makes them interested in the message too.
- **The tale of two films** (`00:40:31–00:44:32`). DSM and AkzoNobel had the same brief, budget and timeline.
  - DSM had a brave client who protected the script ("the script we wrote was the script we shot"). Result: 1.4 million organic views, awards, the McKee mention, new business a decade later.
  - AkzoNobel suffered "design by democracy": changing stakeholders each added messaging. The 90% story became 80, 70, 60… "a Frankenstein monster of corporate objectives", "all message and no soul", forgotten.
- **Root cause: insecurity** (`00:45:57–00:47:09`). A manager afraid the boss won't see the message; a CEO who can't resist listing achievements. Jasper also blames himself: "my job was to make them feel more secure." You build security by knowing where you're going and spotting problems early.
- **Red flag** (`00:47:11`): no decision-maker who will say "I understand that the story comes first, and I will protect it." Without that, the film faces "a death by 1000 cuts."
- **The 90-10 rule** (`00:47:34–00:48:33`), "the single most important rule in this entire course". 90% of the runtime goes to pure storytelling (the human journey, the struggle, the emotion); the final 10% lands the message. "Your message is the reason you are telling the story, but the story is the only reason your audience will listen to your message."
- **In practice** (`00:48:34–00:49:46`). When handed a 20-slide deck, hand back one sentence: "If the audience could only walk away with one thought or feeling, what would it be?" The hero film is the hook, the top of the funnel; everything else lives on the website and in white papers.

## 4. Storywashing (`00:50:29–01:02:52`)
- **Symptom.** A beautiful story with a message slapped on at the end. Three minutes of momentum are shattered in three seconds. The viewer feels cheated and used, which "actively creates distrust."
- **Good example: P&G "Thank You, Mom"** (2012 Olympics; `00:52:14–00:55:54`). The corporate message is a brag: "we sponsor the Olympics and sell products that help moms run their households." It was translated into a human message: "We see you. We acknowledge your struggle and your sacrifices, and we're proud to help in our own small way." Copywriter Kevin Proudfoot distilled it into "The hardest job in the world is the best job. Thank you, Mom." That leads to "P&G, proud sponsor of moms." "It doesn't feel like an ad. It feels like an ode."
- **Bad examples.** Renault Clio: "the car has been around for as long as their relationship. That's it." Vodafone Kiss: "Good things should last forever" connects, but "Vodafone limitless talk, a good thing" puts the scale completely off: "the story wasn't about talking, it was about kissing."
- **Root cause** (`00:57:41`): teams fall in love with a story or concept first and staple the brand on at the end.
- **The 5-step alignment** (`00:58:08–01:02:52`). Always start with the message and work toward the story.
  1. **Theme.** Write the core message; identify the corporate theme (innovation, sustainability, D&I…).
  2. **Human core value.** "If my organization were a person, what human value would it represent within this theme?" Innovation → perseverance; sustainability → responsibility, parenthood, providing for the next generation; diversity → friendship, belonging. It must be a value you can honestly claim.
  3. **Opposite.** Perseverance ↔ giving up, hopelessness; belonging ↔ loneliness, exclusion. "This tension … is the dramatic axis your story will be built on." To make a point about belonging, tell a story about exclusion.
  4. **Protagonist.** The one for whom the journey from opposite to value is most meaningful. Often an underdog, but most compelling is "someone who is expected to have the core value but is actually struggling with the opposite value": the top expert secretly plagued by insecurity, the person surrounded by people who feels utterly alone.
  5. **Research, research, research.** "Great stories are not invented, they are found." Find real people with real struggles; absorb their experiences, language and idiosyncratic details.
- **Result:** "the story is the message, told in human form."

## 5. Melodrama (`01:02:59–01:16:21`)
- **Symptom.** You skip research and brainstorm the scenes, dialogue and conflict. The result is "the plague of cringey cliches … fake feeling … over-the-top emotion", "every line of dialogue is perfectly on the nose, every plot point is predictable. It has the shape of a story, but it has no soul." The audience loses respect.
- **Own failure** (`01:04:10–01:05:36`): a horticulture-sector film built as a "prodigal daughter" arc (a consultant returns to the family farm) with invented motivations, dialogue and scenes: "a skeleton with no blood, no guts, no life."
- "True stories can only be found … trying to invent one … is like an artist trying to paint a portrait of a person they've never met." "It's probably like asking AI to come up with a story for you." (`01:05:38–01:06:26`)
- **Architecture of a true story** (`01:06:26–01:12:31`), "the most important theoretical lesson in this entire course". Every true story centres on **two decisions**, one that gets you into trouble and one that gets you out, with a **dark night of the soul** in between.
  - Jasper's example: a breakup, an all-night failure to write a paper, an F, missing the LSE GPA cutoff, complaining to the dean, shame. That was the dark night.
  - Jung: the place "where the ego goes to die … transformation demands destruction."
  - The ego wanted LSE (external validation); the soul needed to study what he loved. Decision 2: stay in Utrecht and do psychology.
  - "The meaning of the story, the catharsis … is the insight you gain in that darkness."
- **The dark night is a story beacon** (`01:10:33–01:12:31`). Ask people for a story and you get "a polished, boring success anecdote"; ask for times they really doubted themselves and you find a true story. For Canon: "When have you experienced a moment in your career where you felt extreme insecurity?"
- **The five research questions** (`01:13:10–01:15:17`):
  1. When did you doubt yourself the most? This locates the dark night; its feeling should match the opposite value.
  2. Which decision did you doubt most then? This is Decision 1, ego-driven.
  3. Why did you make it, and what did you hope to achieve? This is the want.
  4. What was the real reason you wanted that goal? This reveals the ego that had to be confronted.
  5. What did you realize about yourself that let you get out of trouble, and what was that decision? This is Decision 2: the meaning, the lesson, the point.
  - Then dig for scenes: what triggered Decision 1; the struggles (physical: the world against you; interpersonal: people against you); where, who, what it felt like. "They will come naturally."
  - "You are not inventing a story. You are digging for story … filled with authentic, quirky, unpredictable details." "You are simply reporting on an emotional truth that already existed."
- **Use it on yourself** (`01:15:19`): for speeches, About pages, LinkedIn posts, "look for your dark nights of the soul."

## 6. Bragging (`01:16:47–01:34:17`)
- **Symptom.** A story stripped of conflict and struggle: "the protagonist wants something, makes a plan, and then succeeds without any real obstacles." It is a humble brag; "a story where nothing difficult happens is not a story at all."
- **Root cause: nega-phobia**, the fear that showing struggle or failure makes the brand look bad.
- **Three story types** (`01:17:58–01:20:04`):
  - **Anecdote**: "just some stuff that happened". No meaning, no decisions, no dark night. Fine to tell, but no impact.
  - **Accomplishment**: goal, decide, succeed. Bragging, "the vast majority of all corporate and marketing films", resented by audiences.
  - **True story**: the only one that works.
- **Why we brag** (`01:20:05–01:21:38`): "it's more comfortable." One decision alone makes "obnoxious bragging." Jasper lists his own failures (losses, bad films, firing the wrong people) and notes that respecting the client's discomfort is "the 1st step into helping your client tell a true story."
- **The One Camera story graph** (`01:22:16–01:24:54`), built for "short message-oriented stories … a couple of minutes long":
  - Main character; something missing or needing to change, about to be revealed.
  - **Inciting incident**: something external that reveals it and clarifies the goal (object of desire, want).
  - **Debate**: is it worth stepping into the unknown? "If it were easy, it wouldn't be interesting."
  - **Decision 1** begins Act 2. "Instead, the opposite of what the protagonist expects happens: conflict or struggle."
  - **Conflict on 3 levels**: external/physical (the world and the elements), interpersonal (people), internal (self-doubt).
  - The accumulation leads to the **dark night**: intense doubt, "the moment where transformation begins … What they want is not the same as what they actually need."
  - **Decision 2**: "the reborn protagonist makes the 2nd crucial decision not to give up and to pursue the goal in a renewed way."
  - **Act 3**: the protagonist achieves the goal and reveals the message. "Achieving the goal represents the connection between the story and the sender's message, providing the perfect setup for … the logo and the payoff."
- **Graph rules** (`01:29:42–01:32:42`):
  - The struggle must be real, ideally on all three layers, each more intense; "the deepest being inner struggle." "Life is hard, and in a story, life should be at its absolute hardest."
  - Snyder: "a whiff of death" at the bottom. No actual death is needed, but death "should somehow be around the corner or be sensed." "The deeper the valley … the more triumphant … the peak."
  - **Storify**: "a story is not … actual life … but a distilled metaphor for life. We strip away the trivial and select only those moments when something changes, when values shift, when expectations collide with reality."
  - **Not a rule book**: a flash-forward at the start ("always worth considering", especially for a 5-second skippable pre-roll), starting in the middle of the dark night, Memento-style reverse order, Pulp Fiction chopping. "The graph is the box. Feel free to think outside it."
- "Don't be nega-phobic. Embrace the dark side … The real risk is being boring. And in today's world, being boring is a death sentence." (`01:32:43`)

## 7. Formats (`01:34:23–01:49:52`)
- Formats are a typology that sets expectations, selects reference films, briefs the crew and gives a shared language for **budget, creative approach and emotional outcome**. "Showing a relevant example film is the single most powerful way to convince a client."
- **Three binary questions:**
  1. **Goal: inform or emotional experience?** You can't do both: "information pushes out emotion." To inform, you need existing standing with the audience, or their interest in the topic.
  2. **Audience: internal/captive or external/broad and skeptical?** The more skeptical the audience, the more the film serves their needs (emotion, entertainment) over the sender's. Door A = **pull film** (cut what we want to say to keep them); door B = **push film** (say everything even if some drop off).
  3. **Message: scripted (word for word) or unscripted (emerging from interviews)?** Unscripted is generally cheaper and slower; the real choice is whether you'll pay more for control and speed.
- **Emotional family:**
  - **Hero film**: scripted, pull, actors, multiple locations, cinematic score, 100% emotional impact; most control, most expensive; "the 90/10 rule is really the law here." If the horsemen get in, it becomes an **identity film**: push, "a voiceover that probably doesn't even represent a character", story in free fall.
  - **Human interest film**: documentary, unscripted, pull, built on one powerful real interview; often 5–10× cheaper; less control over the final message, "as it must arise naturally from what your subject says"; needs deep trust and a safe space. If watered down, it becomes an **impression film**: a mood film, "a nice impression."
- **Informational family:** the **explainer film** and **theme doc** (unscripted; improved by framing around a protagonist with a problem and the product as the tool); the **report film** (unscripted, event-based) and the **statement film** (scripted, to camera). These are workhorses where the message is king, but "a little bit of story goes a long way."
- "A format is not a rigid box … it's a strategic starting point." Limitations boost creativity.

## 8. Crafting kama muta (`01:50:07–02:00:11`)
- **The KLM story** (`01:50:07–01:54:48`) is Jasper's own reunion moment (see course-films.md). "This would have been a much less interesting story if it weren't for the kamamuta moment at the end … That moment took all the struggle, all the intention, all the obstacles from the story and converted it to raw emotions." "If there's one thing I'd like you to take away from this course, it's to have an idea of how to craft a kamamuta scene at the end of your story."
- "Creating kamamuta is not magic." Understand the triggers and engineer scenes to hit them. **The five ingredients** (`01:54:49–01:58:59`):
  1. **Empathy.** "You cannot feel kamamuta for a character you don't care about." Save the cat: early on, "a small act of kindness or a moment of vulnerability or a display of endearing humor." Examples: DSM protagonists presenting plans and asking for funding; P&G moms' quiet devotion; the Renault girls shown first as anxious little girls.
  2. **Suddenness.** "The connection shouldn't steadily increase." It comes as "a twist, a turn, or a reveal", like the doctor suddenly reunited with his patient or the lover showing up at her doorstep (Renault). "It doesn't need to be unexpected … certainly it shouldn't be a deus ex machina. But it should be sudden. Your story must build tension and then release it in a sudden, surprising moment of grace or redemption, usually involving people suddenly meeting each other or coming together or being reunited."
  3. **Physical connection.** "Direct your actors or subjects to physically come together": hugs, handshakes, dancing, moving in sync, shared laughter, a comforting hand on a shoulder, applause, a celebratory high five, "a universal language of community." Examples: the disabled woman standing up in bionic legs and hugging her wife (DSM); the medical staff coming together with their patient (Canon).
  4. **Physical manifestations.** Because kama muta is felt in the third person, "show your characters experiencing it themselves … capture it on their faces": tears welling (the P&G mothers after their children's wins), "the shiver run down their spine", "the uncontainable joyful laugh", "a hint at the goosebumps." "When we see another human being being visibly moved, we are moved with them. It's a chain reaction of empathy."
  5. **Reunion or redemption arcs.** P&G is "a series of mini reunions between mother and child after every event." "Pretty much every Volvo ad is a redemption story. A young family is saved from a tragic injustice, a car accident."
- **Application by form** (`01:59:00–02:00:11`):
  - Documentary: ask questions that may lead subjects to get emotional, or "set up a surprise meeting or a reunion for them, like we did in the Canon film. Just be ready to capture that real-life moment and those real tears."
  - Scripted: "brief your actors about what kamamuta is and workshop it with them. Make them recollect similar moments in their own experience."
  - Push formats: still include kama muta elements "or the suggestion thereof."

## 9. Managing the process (`02:00:12–02:11:13`)
- **The pitch**: sell "a strategic solution to your client's most pressing … business challenge … reputation." Ask the three questions, then the binary versions. Show reference films per choice; formats double as a budget proxy.
- **The moment of truth.** "Why should I pay for the true storytelling format when it will even limit the amount of messaging I can put in?" Answer: "The push film will tick all the boxes with your boss, except for one, the actual goal of your film, reputation impact." Cite the University of Amsterdam data; "the difference between being seen and being remembered"; "scientifically backed return on emotion." "You're not upselling a product, you're upselling an outcome."
- Show a reference film and watch their eyes: "moist … or a soft gasp … you're in." You may borrow other people's films while starting out, with credit.
- **Tight–Loose–Tight** (`02:06:38–02:08:49`):
  - Tight (briefing to a signed pre-production plan): story, format, message, arc, casting, locations, budget, schedule. This is "your shield … the constitution."
  - Loose (shoot through first edit): trust the crew, keep the client in the loop.
  - Tight (consolidated feedback through one channel).
  - When a note threatens the core, pull out the shield and hold the line, "from a place of confidence."
- "A great film can only be made with a brave client." Clients called these films "the best project in their whole career."

## 10. Why it matters (`02:11:28–02:24:05`)
- **Jasper's own story.** The DSM film lost money and ended in a fight with his director Hugo. He then spent five years on small films (500 a year) that he never showed his family; in 2018 the company ran at a loss and he doubted himself. In 2019 the US Farmers & Ranchers Alliance CEO asked for a film like DSM's. He called Hugo after five years, apologized, they wrote it together, and his spark was reignited (see course-films.md, Texas farmer).
- **"What do you have to say to the world?"** (`02:19:30–02:20:06`). After analysing the client's message, ask yourself this; the film is best when the maker puts their heart and soul and "your view of life into the story."
- Our purpose "is to make people feel something, and the most special, most valuable feeling we can possibly create is a sense of connection" (Maslow, right after food and shelter). Be "lighthouses" in the storm of soulless AI content; "look for the dark nights of the soul because that's where the real meaning is forged."
